Main Findings from descriptive analysis:
1. The final dataset contains 34,123 complete hourly observations and 45 columns.
2. The data covers almost four years, from August 2022 to June 2026.
3. There are no missing values, duplicate timestamps, or hourly gaps.
4. The average AQI is approximately 93, while the median is 83.
5. The AQI distribution is likely positively skewed due to occasional severe pollution episodes.
6. PM2.5 has the strongest positive correlation with AQI at approximately 0.725.
7. SO₂, CO, NO₂, and PM10 also show meaningful positive correlations.
8. Wind speed and wind gusts are negatively associated with AQI, possibly because stronger winds disperse pollutants.
9. Temperature, humidity, and dew point also show negative relationships with AQI.
10. Precipitation has almost no overall linear correlation with AQI.
11. The highest recorded AQI was 297 on 9 October 2022.
12. The lowest AQI observation and its extremely low particulate values should be inspected for possible anomaly behaviour.
13. Correlation represents association, not causation.
14. Low-correlation features should not be removed until model performance and nonlinear relationships are examined.

AQI over time graph:
15. The AQI time series shows strong temporal variability.
16. AQI generally stays around moderate levels but experiences repeated short-term spikes.
17. Extreme AQI events are clearly visible, especially in late 2022 and early 2023.
18. The graph suggests that AQI behaviour is not random and likely contains seasonal and temporal structure.
19. The visual pattern supports the use of lag features, rolling features, and time-based features in the forecasting model.
20. The graph also supports the statistical finding that the AQI distribution is right-skewed due to occasional severe pollution events.

AQI Distribution:
21. AQI is right-skewed.
22. Most observations lie around 60–110.
23. The highest frequency appears around 70–90.
24. Mean AQI is greater than the median because of high-value pollution events.
25. The distribution is not symmetric or normally distributed.
26. Rare high-AQI observations create a long right tail extending toward 300.
27. Extreme events may be harder for the model to predict because they are less common.
28. High-AQI cases should be evaluated separately during model testing.
29. Extreme observations should not be removed without evidence that they are erroneous.

Average AQI by hour of day:
30. The graph reveals a clear daily AQI cycle.
31. AQI remains fairly stable from early morning to around midday.
32. AQI begins to rise in the afternoon.
33. The highest average AQI occurs in the evening, around 19:00.
34. AQI decreases again after the evening peak.
35. The difference between the lowest and highest average hourly AQI is roughly 9 units.
36. This pattern supports the inclusion of hour as a predictive feature.
37. Later, cyclical encoding of the hour feature may improve model performance.

Average AQI by day of week:
38. AQI varies only slightly across the days of the week.
39. Weekly variation is much weaker than hourly variation.
40. Friday appears to have the lowest average AQI.
41. Sunday and Monday appear among the highest.
42. There is no clear evidence that weekends have lower AQI.
43. day_of_week and is_weekend should remain candidate features until their model contribution is tested.

Average AQI by month:
44. AQI shows a strong seasonal pattern.
45. January has the highest average AQI.
46. November and December are also highly polluted.
47. May has the lowest average AQI.
48. Cooler months generally show worse air quality.
49. The seasonal difference is around 43 AQI units.
50. Monthly variation is much stronger than weekday variation.
51. The result strongly supports keeping month as a forecasting feature.
52. Cyclical encoding of month may improve model performance.
53. A year-by-month comparison should be added later to test whether the seasonal pattern is consistent across years.
54. Generally monsoon or summer vacation months show low aqi.

Correlation Heatmap:
55. PM2.5 has the strongest positive relationship with AQI.
56. Major pollutants tend to increase together during pollution episodes.
57. Carbon monoxide and nitrogen dioxide are strongly correlated.
58. PM10 and dust show a strong positive relationship.
59. Temperature, humidity, and dew point contain overlapping meteorological information.
60. Wind speed and wind gusts are very strongly correlated.
61. Surface pressure is positively related to AQI and negatively related to several weather variables.
62. Ozone shows a different relationship pattern from primary pollutants.
63. Precipitation has weak overall linear relationships.
64. The dataset contains multicollinearity, which should be considered when interpreting Ridge Regression coefficients.
65. Correlated features should not be removed solely from the heatmap; their effect on validation performance should be tested.

PM2.5 against AQI:
66. PM2.5 has a clear positive relationship with AQI.
67. The relationship is strong but nonlinear.
68. PM2.5 is likely one of the most important forecasting features.
69. Similar PM2.5 values can still produce very different AQI values.
70. Other pollutants and weather features are therefore necessary.
71. A visible concentration occurs around AQI 150.
72. Extreme PM2.5 observations are rare and should be inspected rather than automatically removed.
73. Random Forest may model this relationship better than Ridge Regression.

Modelling Implications:
Keep PM2.5 as a core model feature.
Use nonlinear models such as Random Forest.
Still compare against Ridge Regression as a baseline.
Do not rely on PM2.5 alone.
Evaluate model performance during extreme pollution periods.
Consider whether outliers disproportionately affect RMSE.

PM10 against AQI:
74. PM10 has a moderate positive relationship with AQI.
75. Its relationship is weaker than that of PM2.5.
76. There is substantial spread at similar PM10 values.
77. PM10 alone cannot explain AQI.
78. The relationship is nonlinear.
79. Extreme PM10 observations exist above 400–600.
80. These extreme values should be inspected, not automatically deleted.
81. The AQI 150 concentration appears again.
82. PM10 should remain in the model as a secondary particulate feature.

Modelling Implications:
Keep PM10 as a feature.
Do not treat it as the primary pollutant predictor.
PM2.5 is likely more informative.
Random Forest may handle the nonlinear and scattered pattern better.
Outliers should be examined separately.
PM10 may still add useful information when combined with dust, weather, and PM2.5.

Wind speed against AQI:
83. Wind speed has a negative relationship with AQI.
84. Severe AQI values mainly occur under low or moderate wind speeds.
85. At high wind speeds, AQI is generally lower and less variable.
86. The relationship appears nonlinear.
87. Wind likely assists pollutant dispersion.
88. Wind speed alone does not explain AQI because there is substantial spread at similar speeds.
89. Wind direction should later be encoded using sine and cosine.
90. The horizontal cluster of very low AQI observations should be inspected as a possible unusual period.

Relative humidity against aqi:
91. Relative humidity has a weak negative correlation with AQI.
92. Higher humidity is generally associated with slightly lower AQI.
93. The relationship is highly scattered.
94. Humidity alone cannot explain air-quality variation.
95. High AQI events still occur at high humidity levels.
96. The relationship may be nonlinear and season-dependent.
97. Vertical bands are caused by humidity being recorded as integer percentages.
98. Humidity should remain a candidate feature, but it is probably less important than PM2.5, AQI lags, and seasonal features.
